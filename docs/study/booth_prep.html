02Three insights from the CeraVe ad

The CeraVe Super Bowl ad, in its 30-second broadcast cut and 76-second extended cut. Things you can't tell just by watching it:

  1. Sound off cuts its response almost in half. The 30-second cut loses 46% on mute, and its opening hook is his voice: muted, the first two seconds fall below typical. Most social feeds start muted, so the hook has to work in the pictures or captions.
  2. The long cut wastes the seconds that decide a skip. The 76-second cut opens on 8 seconds of dark curtains, below typical, and YouTube lets viewers skip an ad after 5. The AI report's fix: show his face or the product sooner.
  3. The AI report finds the weak shot and says what to change. In the 30-second cut, one wide board-room shot with no faces (19–23 s) is the weakest stretch; the report suggests cutting to a close-up of someone reacting. In the long cut it flags the CGI narwhal (47–52 s) and suggests cutting back to him.

03The industry, in three points

  1. Ad testing is moving from panels to AI. Big firms (Kantar, System1, Realeyes) test ads on real viewers, which takes hours to weeks. Newer tools (Neurons, Dragonfly AI) use AI to predict where people look, in seconds. Neuromarketing is about a USD 1.5 billion market.
  2. Their real strength is proof. They have checked their scores against the results of hundreds of thousands of real ads. What we adopted: we keep a fixed baseline (0 = typical viewing) so videos can be compared, and we already collect proof: users who add their video's audience-retention curve can get its price back as credit. Checking our curves against those is the obvious next step.
  3. Nobody sells what we do. The AI tools predict attention, where eyes go. We predict the whole brain response, split into seeing, hearing and understanding, plus sound-off and side-by-side cuts, self-serve in minutes. The brain model came out in March 2026 and is licensed for non-commercial use only, so NeuroLens is a proof of concept, not a product for sale.

04Three limits, and what we'd do

It's a prediction, not yet proven on ads

No one's brain was scanned watching your ad, and we haven't checked our scores against real ad results.

Next: compare our curves with the YouTube retention curves users share.

It shows an average viewer

The model learned from people watching film and TV, not your target audience.

Next: retrain on ad viewers, or by audience, once there's data.

A brain response isn't sales

Results also depend on targeting, budget, timing, the product and luck. We measure one ingredient: how strongly the video itself engages.

Use it to pick and fix cuts before launch, alongside real campaign tests.

05Quick answers

Is this real brain data?
Predicted, by a model trained on real brain scans of people watching film and TV.
How long does it take?
About 4 minutes for a 30-second clip. If nobody has used it for a while, add about 10 minutes while the GPU switches on.
Why a dollar?
A 30-second clip costs us roughly 35 to 65 cents (estimate): about 15 cents of GPU time, a few cents for the AI report (about 14 cents for a 2-minute video), plus the GPU's 7-minute idle tail before it switches off, which busy hours share. $1.00 per 30 seconds covers that, and refunds when a job fails.
What happens to my video?
Deleted after 30 days, or whenever you delete it. Visitors' uploads from today are deleted after the event; a video shared for research can be kept up to 3 years.
How is it built?
All on AWS: serverless website and database that pause when idle, a GPU that starts when a video arrives and stops 7 minutes after the last one, credit refunded automatically if anything fails.
